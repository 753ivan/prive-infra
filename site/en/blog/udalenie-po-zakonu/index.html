Your photo is your property: how content is removed by law

How platform liability for third-party content works in the US, the EU and Ukraine, who owns the rights to a photo, what a notice must contain, how quickly platforms respond — and where the mechanism stops working.

The legal mechanism for compelled content removal: intermediary liability, the rights holder’s notice and platform response times

There are two fundamentally different ways to get an image removed from the internet. The first is a complaint about a breach of community guidelines: the platform reviews it at its own discretion, on a timeline it sets itself, and with no obligations to the complainant. The second is a legally significant notice that moves the relationship into the realm of liability: once it receives a properly drafted notice, an intermediary loses its immunity from liability for third-party content if it fails to act. Below: how the second mechanism works in the US, the European Union and Ukraine, what a notice must contain, how quickly platforms respond, and where the mechanism stops working.

The legal framework: an intermediary’s “safe harbour”

Neither US nor European law requires platforms to check everything users publish in advance. Instead, there’s conditional immunity: an intermediary isn’t liable for content posted by third parties as long as it doesn’t know the content is unlawful — and it keeps that immunity only if, once it learns, it promptly removes the material or disables access to it. A legal notice is exactly how you create that “knowledge”.

Who owns the rights to a photo

Copyright arises the moment a work is created and requires no registration — a principle set out in Article 5(2) of the Berne Convention, to which both Ukraine and Georgia are parties. The key question, however, is a different one: the rights holder is the author of the photo, not the person shown in it. That creates a fork in the road that determines everything that follows.

Required elements of a notice

A DMCA notice must contain six elements listed in § 512(c)(3)(A). Article 16(2) of the DSA sets comparable requirements: an explanation of why the content is illegal, the exact location, the notifier’s name and email, and a statement of good faith. Missing any element gives the provider formal grounds to disregard the notice.

Liability for bad-faith notices is real: § 512(f) allows damages against anyone who knowingly misrepresents facts in a notice, and Article 23 of the DSA lets platforms suspend processing notices from those who repeatedly file manifestly unfounded ones. So only file notices for material you genuinely hold the rights to.

Four limitations the “cleaners” won’t tell you about

01

Counter-notice

Whoever posted the material can challenge the removal. Then under § 512(g) the provider must restore the content after 10–14 business days unless the rights holder files a lawsuit in that time. In practice, distributors almost never challenge removals: a counter-notice requires disclosing their name and address and consenting to a court’s jurisdiction.

02

Your name in a public database

Google sends copies of legal notices to the public Lumen database. The notifier’s contact details aren’t shared, but Lumen publishes the rights holder’s name as it appears in the notice. If you’re concealing your identity, file through an agent and agree on how the name is written in advance.

03

De-indexing is not removal

Google’s decision removes the link from results, but the page stays accessible on the site. Full removal is only achieved through the site owner, the hosting provider or a court. So both tracks run in parallel: de-indexing cuts off the audience; a demand to the source eliminates the material.

04

Where the mechanism doesn’t work

Private chats and personal messages, “bulletproof” hosts, services that ignore notices and are beyond the reach of US and European jurisdiction. Different tools apply here: de-indexing in search engines, StopNCII for major platforms, demands to the domain registrar and criminal proceedings.
