Why this winter

01

For a holiday, for the winter or for good

For the winter you need neither a residence permit nor a work permit – just insurance, a rental and money

Georgia is one of the easiest countries to go to for the winter: an international passport, insurance – and a few hours later you’re in Tbilisi, where there’s electricity, no air-raid sirens, and you can dance until morning. Wintering and relocating are different modes with different rules. For the winter you need no residence permit, no work permit, no sole-trader registration – just insurance, a lease and money. All the paperwork starts where winter turns into a year, and it’s collected at the very end of the guide.

Two voices of 2026
Marharyta moved to Tbilisi from Poland: “we’re surrounded by great, happy people, we’re not afraid to say we’re from Ukraine” – her only regret is not coming sooner. Dasha, who moved from Berlin, says the “rose-tinted glasses came off”: few goods in the shops, chaos on the roads and a border where you can be turned back without explanation. Both are right: the first about people, the second about the system. This guide is about both.

02

Your Tbilisi: brunch, wine, techno until morning

Cafés, restaurants, clubs, day trips – and what of it works in winter

Put simply: by day Kyiv wins, by night Tbilisi does. Kyiv has more choice, a higher bar for service and prettier interiors, but everything closes at 11 pm and the clubs live on “daytime raves”. Dnipro is cosy, with a good embankment and food, but no real club scene and air-raid alerts almost every night. Tbilisi has less polish and more atmosphere, and for the first time in years you can dance until morning without checking the clock.

What it feels like in practice. In Tbilisi, a Saturday for a woman from Kyiv looks like this: a late brunch at Stamba or Fabrika, the flea market by the Dry Bridge and vintage shops in Vera in the afternoon, a wine bar in Sololaki in the evening, Bassiani until dawn after midnight. Sunday – a car to Kakheti or the sulphur baths. All of it costs about the same as in Kyiv.

What you’ll miss: Kyiv-level service (in Georgia a waiter can disappear for 20 minutes), 15-minute delivery, big malls and your own circle – you’ll have to build it from scratch, including in Tbilisi’s Ukrainian chats.

The social code. Young people in Tbilisi are just like Kyivans: English, techno, tattoos, pro-European. But the older generation and districts like Gldani live by different rules: a short dress and a cigarette on the street will draw looks, and renting together with a boyfriend will raise questions from the landlady. Dating – Tinder and Bumble work, the crowd is expats, IT people and Tbilisi bohemia; Georgian men from traditional families rarely bring a foreign woman home – worth understanding in advance.

03

After Kyiv and Dnipro: an honest comparison

The gain isn’t price – it’s safety

In short: in terms of comfort, Tbilisi is roughly Kyiv without air-raid alerts, but with worse roads and air. Batumi is a resort town the size of a district centre – empty in winter and crowded in summer. In terms of infrastructure, Dnipro is closer to Tbilisi than to Batumi, but in Dnipro the war is close every day.

Key point
Tbilisi has almost all the work, nightlife and healthcare; Batumi has the sea and calm, but it’s seasonal. Rent in central Tbilisi is already comparable to Kyiv.

Money-wise there’s almost no difference: rent and salaries in Tbilisi and Kyiv are comparable. Georgia’s advantage isn’t that it’s cheap – it’s safety, and the fact that there’s always electricity and water.

Housing costs about the same; the metro is half the price of Kyiv’s after the fare rose to 30 UAH; but cafés, salons and restaurants are more expensive. A Kyivan’s budget doesn’t shrink in Tbilisi – it shifts towards “going out”.

Lypky and premium Vake are in the same price league: a one-bedroom in Axis Towers costs as much as an average one-bedroom on Verkhohliada Street, and only Kyiv’s most expensive listings ($3–5k) have no equivalent in Tbilisi – the market there tops out at ~$2,400. Starokozatska 52 is a solid central Dnipro address, not premium: for the same $400–650 in Tbilisi you’ll get Saburtalo or the edge of Vake, but not a tower with a view. Moving from Dnipro “at the same level”, budget for rent two to three times higher; from Lypky – about the same.

04

Money: how far it goes

Georgia hasn’t been cheap for a while

In winter, one person in Tbilisi needs about $1,000 a month including rent to live modestly, about $1,500 to live comfortably and about $1,700 in the centre. A family with a child – from $2,000. Groceries and restaurants cost about the same as in Kyiv, sometimes more. Plus one-off costs at the start: a month’s deposit and insurance.

The difference between “modest” and “in the centre” is almost twofold, and nearly all of it comes from two items: rent and cafés. Groceries and utilities change little whichever district you choose.

05

Tbilisi in 4 days: take a look

Not sights, but three decisions: district, flat, budget

Go on a scouting trip before you move your things. Four days is enough to see two or three districts, view real flats rather than photos, go to the baths and a bar, and understand whether this is your city. November or early December is best: you’ll see the real winter Tbilisi – gas boiler, grey sky, short days – and have time to rent a flat before 15 December, before the market freezes for the holidays.

Key point
Book flat viewings and meetings before you fly – Tbilisi’s pace is slower than Kyiv’s, and “let’s do it tomorrow” here means “sometime this week”.

Before you fly

  • Insurance is mandatory even for 4 days; it’s checked at boarding and at the border.
  • List 10–15 flats on myhome.ge and ss.ge in three districts (Vera/Sololaki, Vake, Saburtalo) and message the owners: half won’t reply, and half of the rest will rent before you arrive – that’s normal.
  • Join the Telegram chats of Ukrainians in Tbilisi and ask about commission-free housing and trusted realtors.
  • Stay in Vera or Sololaki for these days – where you’re aiming to live, not by the station.
  • Save these numbers in your phone: 112 and the Embassy of Ukraine +995 595 082 288.

What to check in a flat. Heating: a gas boiler (“karma”) in older buildings – 150–250 GEL (≈ $55–95) a month in winter; central heating is almost non-existent; windows and damp – mould in the corners and ground floors of old buildings; water pressure and the boiler; lift and floor – the old centre often has no lift; internet – ask whether Magti or Silknet fibre is installed; noise – the bars of Vera and Sololaki stay open until morning; parking, if you plan to have a car. Contract: you need one in your name even for 3 months – the bank will ask for it, and if winter turns into a move, you can’t apply for residence without it. Many owners don’t want a contract because of tax – ask about it before the viewing, not after. Check the deposit, payment currency (usually USD) and who pays the utilities. A realtor’s fee is 50–100% of a month’s rent; through chats and directly – no fee. More on winter housing and real listings in the “Winter housing” section.

What to check in a district. How long a taxi to the centre takes in the evening and what it costs ($3–6 is normal); whether there’s a metro within a 10-minute walk; a shop, pharmacy and market nearby; air – Saburtalo and Vake are higher and cleaner, while the lowland by the river is smoggy in winter; street lighting in the evening, if you plan to come home alone; dogs – street packs are vaccinated and usually calm, but many find them stressful.

Scouting budget. Accommodation $50–90 a night in the centre, food $30–50 a day, taxis and metro $15–20 for the whole stay, baths and an evening at a bar $50–80. In total $450–750 per person for 4 days excluding flights – the cheapest mistake you can make when relocating.

The test
If after four days you can’t name a district, a rent figure and a move-in date, it’s better to postpone wintering than to “figure it out on the spot”.
06

The border: what you need and how long you can stay

For Ukrainian citizens

Ukrainian citizens enter visa-free and can stay in the country for one full year from the date of entry (a resolution of 2 April 2025 cut the period from three years to one). Those who entered before 24 February 2025 have had their stay extended to 24 February 2027 (Resolution No. 80 of 24.02.2026).

Key point
A year visa-free on an international passport, but insurance for the whole period is mandatory – it’s checked as early as boarding. Your entry date is the starting point for everything else.

What you need at the border

  • An international passport (the domestic ID card won’t do).
  • Medical insurance – mandatory since 1 January 2026 for all foreigners, for the entire stay. Checked at boarding and at the border.
    • What you need: cover from 30,000 GEL (≈ $11,000) for hospitalisation and accidents. Look beyond the overall limit: cheap policies cover hospitalisation only up to 6,500 GEL (≈ $2,400).
    • Cost: a travel policy from ~$1 a day ($0.75–1.10 with Georgian insurers), a local annual policy $300–400. Without a policy – a 300 GEL (≈ $110) fine and possible refusal of entry.
    • What people do in practice: a 1–2 week visitor policy is enough for the border – GeoCover, Unison and others sell it online in a few minutes, even from the departure airport. A longer policy is bought locally: a broken arm without insurance can cost up to 10,000 GEL. Better not to show “bank card insurance” at the border – you need a clear policy with the cover amount.
  • Recommended: proof of accommodation and funds – the border officer decides on entry each time.

Important details

  • Re-entering formally resets the year, but “visa runs” aren’t guaranteed: the decision is made at the border, and refusals became more common in 2025–2026 – even IT workers who had lived like that for two years were turned back: “you had enough time to get a residence permit.” If you do go: from Tbilisi – to Armenia via Sadakhlo–Bagratashen (there and back in a day; people find travel companions in the chats); from Batumi and Kobuleti – to Turkey via Sarpi (a “shopping visa run” to Hopa – 60 GEL return). At the border, say it as it is – “re-entry”; don’t leave it to the last 5 days, so you have a buffer in case of refusal.
  • In 2026 airports started asking people without residence permits for a return ticket and an address – have an accommodation booking and any outbound ticket dated within the year.
  • When leaving Ukraine via Moldova, the Ukrainian exit stamp is often not put in – the Moldovans stamp you instead. It doesn’t affect your year in Georgia, but for EU paperwork later you’ll need a certificate of border crossing from Ukraine’s Border Guard Service.
  • Overstay fines have gone up; an entry ban can be issued together with the fine.
  • You must not have Abkhazia or South Ossetia stamps in your passport or enter through uncontrolled checkpoints – that’s a criminal case in Georgia.
  • The free healthcare package for Ukrainians who fled the war was extended until 1 April 2026; check what happens next with Georgia’s Health Ministry or the Embassy of Ukraine.
  • Ukrainian driving licences are valid (Vienna Convention). A car with Ukrainian plates can be imported temporarily; deadlines and duties are on the Revenue Service website (rs.ge).
  • Pets: an international pet passport, a microchip, a rabies vaccination.
  • Embassy of Ukraine in Georgia: hotline +995 595 082 288, consular section +995 322 311 454, emb_ge@mfa.gov.ua; Ukraine’s Foreign Ministry 24/7 line +380 44 238 16 57. Save the numbers in your phone before you fly.

Winter in Tbilisi and Batumi, honestly

Warmer than Kyiv’s, but with cold houses, smog and short days

Tbilisi in winter is not Kyiv. +4…+8 °C by day, around zero at night, −3…−6 in a cold January week; snow falls a few times a winter and melts within a day, and recent winters have been almost dry. January is the driest month of the year, with more sunny days than Kyiv. But there are three things Kyiv doesn’t prepare you for: cold houses, smog and short days. There’s almost no central heating – a gas boiler or air conditioner does the job, the old buildings of Vera and Sololaki are draughty, and +17 indoors when it’s +6 outside is normal unless you spend 150–250 GEL a month on heating. By February the air in the lowland by the river stagnates, and Tbilisi regularly ranks among Europe’s worst cities for air quality – Vake and Saburtalo are higher up and breathe easier. The sun rises at 8:30 and sets at 18:00.

Batumi in winter is a different story: warmer (+8…+12 by day), but from November to April it rains for days on end, wind blows off the sea, there are storms, and half the city is closed until May; new builds by the sea stand empty and cold, and you’ll heat with an air conditioner. Wintering in Batumi only makes sense if you love the sea in any weather and quiet. For your first winter in Georgia, choose Tbilisi.

On the other hand, winter here has things Kyiv’s doesn’t. The ski season at all resorts opens on 20 December and runs until mid-April: Gudauri is 120 km and 2.5 hours from Tbilisi, a minibus from Didube 20–30 GEL, a day ski pass about 70 GEL, rental 40–80 GEL; Bakuriani is quieter and cheaper (ski pass 55 GEL). The Abanotubani sulphur baths are the city’s greatest pleasure in winter. From mid-December to 7 January the city is strung with lights; 1–2 and 7 January are days off, 14 January is the Old New Year, 19 January is Epiphany; cafés and shops are open on these days, government offices aren’t. Kakheti in winter is empty and mild: wine cellars are open and there are no tourists.

The key question isn’t the view, it’s how the flat is heated

Renting for three to five months works differently from renting for a year: no annual contract, furnished with dishes, and the key question isn’t the view but how the flat is heated. Everyday life: banks, healthcare, transport, language

What really costs money and nerves

Key point
An account at TBC or Bank of Georgia is opened with an international passport, the metro is 1 GEL, dinner for two 100–200 GEL. The most expensive things are rent and private healthcare.

Banks. Ukrainians can open an account without much trouble – unlike Russians, who are vetted for weeks. You need an international passport; sometimes they’ll ask for a lease and source of income. TBC and Bank of Georgia have the best apps, cards in GEL/USD/EUR, and Apple Pay works. Account fees for non-residents start at 10–20 GEL (≈ $4–7) a month. Ukrainian PrivatBank and monobank cards are accepted everywhere, but cash withdrawals carry a fee. From 2026 experience: TBC opens an account in a day, Bank of Georgia takes up to six weeks to review applications and doesn’t approve everyone, and Credo is a workable backup. For a sole-trader registration you also need a legal address – not a registered residence, but an address for correspondence, with the landlord’s consent; that’s exactly why “fixers” offering it for 150–200 GEL hang around the entrance to the Public Service Hall. Stripe can’t be connected to a Georgian sole trader – sort out how you’ll receive foreign payments before you move. And the main rule once your account is open: don’t move money through it in transit – “in and straight out”. Banks treat such transactions as suspicious and block the account, and they aren’t obliged to explain a refusal or a block.

Healthcare. State health insurance is only for citizens. Foreigners pay for treatment: a GP appointment 50–100 GEL (≈ $19–37); dentists are cheaper and better than in Europe. Good clinics: Aversi, Evex, Karavadze. Many doctors speak Russian. Pharmacies sell a lot without a prescription, but antibiotics now require one.

Cosmetic surgery and aesthetics. Tbilisi and Batumi are popular for rhinoplasty and eyelid surgery: a nose job costs $2,500–3,800, three to four times cheaper than in Western Europe. Before paying a deposit, check the clinic’s licence and the surgeon’s certificate (issued by the Health Ministry’s Agency for Regulation of Medical Activities), and only have surgery at a clinic with inpatient facilities. Entry insurance doesn’t cover elective surgery or complications; for 1–2 weeks afterwards don’t fly and don’t stay alone.

Transport. Tbilisi metro and buses cost 1 GEL (≈ $0.40), paid by card or Metromoney. Until summer 2026 public transport in Tbilisi was free for Ukrainian citizens – by showing a Ukrainian passport to the inspector or with a concession Metromoney card from the metro ticket office; in August the chats reported the concession had been cancelled, with no official confirmation – check at the ticket office and budget 1 GEL. Bolt and Yandex Go are cheap, but drivers are aggressive. As a pedestrian, get used to the fact that a car may not stop at a zebra crossing – after the range of goods in shops, it’s the second most common complaint from newcomers. Between cities – minibuses and the Tbilisi–Batumi train (5 hours, 25–60 GEL). A car of your own is only worth it for the mountains – in the city there are traffic jams and no parking.

Getting to Ukraine. There are no direct flights. The fastest route is a flight to Chișinău, Rzeszów or Warsaw and then overland; Wizz Air flies from Kutaisi to Poland. A full day on the road and from $150 one way.

Your Ukrainian passport in Tbilisi. If your international passport expires within the next year, renew it at home before the trip. At the embassy (Oniashvili St. 75) applications are by appointment only via Diia, the status “data sent for verification” can hang for up to 3 months, and collecting a ready passport means a half-day queue. A passport with less than 6 months’ validity also raises questions from border officers on the way back.

Parcels from Ukraine. Nova Post isn’t in Georgia yet – it has branches in Poland, the Baltics, Czechia, Germany and a dozen other countries, but not here, so “send it to Nova Poshta in Tbilisi” won’t work. What does work: Meest ships parcels Ukraine↔Georgia (up to 30 kg, tracking at t.meest-group.com, support via Ukrainian numbers); the local Georgian Post (gpost.ge, main branch on Station Square) accepts international parcels; regular Ukrposhta to Georgia also works and, according to 2026 reviews, is the cheapest and fastest (5 days from Chernihiv to Batumi; collect at a pick-up point when notified); for documents and small items – travellers via the same Telegram chats (“going Kyiv–Tbilisi, have space”). Be careful with “couriers” recommended in the chats: in 2026 this is the main scam – someone DMs you, asks for the sender’s details and a prepayment, then deletes the conversation; pay only on handover. Medicines: Ukrainian prescription drugs (sedatives, hormonal, neurological) often can’t be bought or sent here – couriers won’t take them; bring a supply for your whole stay in the original packaging with a prescription; at the border in Batumi they let it through without questions.

Language. Russian helps with people over 30, English with younger ones. Georgian is difficult, but 50 everyday phrases noticeably change how people treat you. People react warmly to Ukrainian – it immediately sets you apart from Russians in locals’ eyes.

What you can’t buy. The most common everyday complaint of Ukrainian women who moved in 2024–2026 isn’t prices but choice. In an ordinary supermarket meat is often only frozen, shrimp and salmon cost “unbelievable money”, and healthy-eating products are limited to gluten-free flour at best; the same goes for cosmetics and mass-market clothing. A parcel from Ukraine takes a long time and costs about 2,000 UAH per kilo. On the other hand, the same people call seasonal fruit and vegetables the best thing in the country. The practical takeaway: bring a supply of cosmetics and specific foods, and buy meat and vegetables at the market rather than the chains.

Meat and fish – where to go. The main central market is Dezerter Bazaar (by the station): fresh meat cut to order, herbs, cheeses, spices, about a third cheaper than the supermarket; there are fish stalls nearby, but for fresh sea fish and shrimp locals go to the big Agrohub and Carrefour stores or order delivery from fishmongers via Instagram – ordinary chains have less choice at higher prices. Districts have their own markets: Varketili, Samgori, Dighomi. For farm meat and sausage “like at home” – shops run by Ukrainians and Russian-speaking stores; ask for their addresses in the chats. Order fish for New Year and holidays in advance – in season it sells out.

A week as it is. A remote worker’s weekday in Tbilisi starts with a 10–12 GEL coffee at a café in Vera or Sololaki; to the centre – metro or bus for 1 GEL by bank card. Lunch – khinkali at 1.5 GEL each or a set lunch for 25–40 GEL; it’s cheaper to buy the week’s groceries at the market: it’s about a third cheaper than the supermarket.

In the evening – a wine bar in Sololaki or dinner for two for 100–200 GEL; make peace in advance with the fact that a waiter may disappear for 20 minutes. Home – a taxi for $3–6, normal even at 3 am; if the night goes on, Bassiani or KHIDI are open until 8 am.

Weekends – the flea market by the Dry Bridge and vintage shops in Vera, or a car to Kakheti or Gudauri: 1–2 hours’ drive, a night at a wine estate $150–350. In winter add the sulphur baths and a gas heating bill; in summer, the train to Batumi: 5 hours and 25–60 GEL.

Ukrainians in Georgia

How we’re perceived, where the chats are, and where to go for help

The people are on our side; the government isn’t. That’s the main thing to understand about Georgia in 2026. On the street a Ukrainian flag hangs on every other bar, a taxi driver will hear Ukrainian and say “Slava Ukraini”, and Russians in the same bar may be asked to sign a “visa” condemning Putin. Meanwhile, the ruling Georgian Dream party has rolled back EU integration since 2024, is at odds with Kyiv (Ukraine sanctioned Georgian officials in December 2024) and cut Ukrainians’ visa-free stay from three years to one.

Russians, Ukrainians and everyday conflicts: what a woman sees

In short: there are practically no open street clashes of “Ukrainians vs Russians” in Georgia – the conflict here is political, not everyday. On the street you’re among friends: Ukrainian flags on bars, stickers saying “don’t come in if you don’t consider Putin a war criminal”, Bassiani stamps “Russia is an occupier” at the door, and some bars (like Dedaena) ask Russians to sign a “visa” condemning the war in advance, not to speak Russian and “not to argue about politics while drunk”. A Ukrainian woman who says she’s from Ukraine almost always gets a warm welcome.

At the same time, most Georgians are calm and hospitable towards ordinary Russians too – the famous “Georgian nonchalance” and the rule of “minding your own business” work both ways. Russian is the lingua franca here: you’ll hear it everywhere, including from relocants themselves. For a Ukrainian woman this means two things: speaking Russian is safe and normal, but in a firmly pro-Ukrainian place a single word in Ukrainian or “I’m from Ukraine” instantly changes the tone and melts the chill – there’s no need, and no point, in passing yourself off as Russian.

Friction, when it happens, comes from the fringes, not the street: pro-Russian groups like Alt-Info and the Conservative Movement have torn down Ukrainian flags in central Tbilisi, and one Russian tried to rip a flag from a flat’s window. These are isolated incidents, not a pattern; since January 2022, insulting the flag or coat of arms of a state with which Georgia has diplomatic relations carries a 1,000 GEL fine.

How the police behave
The police have been reformed and aren’t corrupt, but in a café row they won’t take “your” side on the basis of nationality or “chase Russians away” for you – it’s the same hands-off stance. For you as a foreigner, the main risk in any escalation isn’t your opponent but your migration record: a fight or a scene involving a foreign woman is grounds for a check and, at worst, deportation, while a local would get away with it. So: skip political arguments with drunk people; if someone harasses or threatens you, call 112 (the police really do come), but no physical confrontation. You’re among friends, but don’t expect Georgia to deal with Russians on your behalf.

How many of us there are. About 30,000 Ukrainian citizens, of whom ~25,000 have refugee status and about 6,300 have residence permits (figures from Prime Minister Kobakhidze, 2026). Of the 220,000 who arrived in 2022, few remain: most moved on to the EU, where there are benefits and jobs. Those who stayed are mostly women with children, remote workers and people with family here.

300 + 45 GEL payments. The social assistance programme for Ukrainian citizens who came because of the war has been extended to 1 January 2027: 300 GEL a month per family plus 45 GEL per person (≈ $125 for one person). Apply in Tbilisi at Nobel Brothers St. 27 (Social Service Agency) with your international passport; a decision takes one to two months, and the money arrives around the 20th. From the chats: men aged 18–60 are usually refused, and a one-day visa run doesn’t cancel the payment. The amount is symbolic but roughly covers a one-bedroom’s winter utilities.

Young people 18–30. If you’re from Kyiv or Dnipro and used to going out – you’ll be fine in Tbilisi. Local young people are pro-European, speak English, stood at the protests in winter 2024–2025 and treat Ukrainians as “one of us”. Going out – Fabrika, bars in Vera and Sololaki, the clubs Bassiani, KHIDI, Left Bank. There’s no curfew – after Kyiv it feels like freedom. What takes getting used to: society as a whole is more conservative – the church is strong, the older generation is patriarchal, and a woman alone at a bar in a simpler district will attract attention. LGBT topics have been banned from public life since the 2024 law.

Work is hard to find. Office salaries without Georgian – $600–900, in services – $400–600. Plus, since March 2026 employers must obtain a work permit for foreigners, and many simply won’t bother. The realistic scenario for a young Ukrainian is remote work for a Ukrainian or European company. Bloggers and creatives should know in advance: the market is small, the audience many times smaller than in Ukraine.

What’s worrying. Since autumn 2025 the migration service has been raiding bars and restaurants in Tbilisi and Batumi; from 1 January to 27 October 2026 more than 3,500 foreigners were deported with an entry ban. Since October 2026 checks of bars and restaurants have intensified, and now Ukrainians are caught too: detainees, even with legal status, are talked into “voluntary self-deportation”, driven to the Sarpi checkpoint and barred from re-entry – sometimes even after paying a fine. Georgia doesn’t let in Ukrainians with criminal records or those Russia deports via Upper Lars: in summer 2026 about 80 such people lived for months in the basement of the Dariali checkpoint. Refusal of entry without explanation is possible even with a residence permit – newcomers call this the country’s biggest downside. Political activism by a foreigner is a direct route to a residence-permit refusal: you can go to protests, but keep out of frame with a flag.

Late November 2026. The opposition is calling for a big rally on 28 November, the second anniversary of the protests; the climax may stretch into the following days. Ukraine’s Foreign Ministry advises Ukrainians to avoid mass gatherings and not to take part. If your first visit falls on these dates, stay in Vake, Saburtalo or Vera rather than near Rustaveli Avenue and parliament, and avoid the centre in the evening. Otherwise the city is calm: in Numbeo’s 2026 survey, walking alone at night scores 70 out of 100 in Tbilisi and 77 in Batumi.

Political outlook for 1–2 years. The government is stable, the opposition is in prison or in exile, and EU talks are frozen until 2028. Meanwhile the economy is growing. For everyday life this means: calm and predictable, but the rules for foreigners will only get stricter.

What you mustn’t do in Georgia

Even if you’d get away with it in Ukraine

The main difference isn’t the laws themselves but that here they work. Since the police reform of the 2000s, the familiar “let’s sort it out on the spot” simply doesn’t exist in Georgia. On top of that, a foreigner faces a second punishment on top of any first one – deportation with an entry ban. You can pay a fine; you can’t pay off an entry ban.

Slipping a patrol officer something to settle it
A separate criminal case for bribery and real prison time. The patrol police are the showcase of the reform, everything is filmed, attempts don’t work
Weed is “almost legal” since use was decriminalised
Use itself has been decriminalised since 2018, but buying, possessing and passing it to a friend haven’t. The law was tightened in 2025; in 9 months of 2026 prosecutors registered 200+ drug cases against Russians alone. Serious offences carry up to 20 years or life
“Treating” friends at a party
Legally that’s dealing, not treating, and it’s classified very differently from personal use
Working unofficially for a couple of months and sorting out papers later
Since 01.03.2026 – work permit first, income second. The migration service checks bars, cafés and delivery; from 1 January to 27 October 2026 more than 3,500 foreigners were deported. Even people with residence permits and property were deported
Overstaying a bit and leaving after paying a small fine
Since autumn 2025: up to 3 months over – 1,000 GEL (≈ $370) and a six-month entry ban; up to a year – 2,000 GEL and 2 years; over a year – 3,000 GEL and 3 years
Having a glass and getting behind the wheel
The limit is 0.3‰ – practically one glass. First offence from 700 GEL (≈ $260), repeat – up to 2,500 GEL and a six-month licence suspension. Patrols stop cars often
Smoking in a bar or on a veranda
Smoking indoors is strictly banned, including vapes and hookahs. The venue is fined 500 GEL, 1,000 GEL for a repeat, so you’ll be shown out without discussion
Going to “see Abkhazia” from the Russian side
A criminal case for illegal border crossing. The stamp in your passport is grounds to refuse you entry to the country altogether
Joining a protest and blocking a street
The fine for blocking a road was raised from 500 to 5,000 GEL (≈ $1,850). For a foreigner, a police report at a protest is a real path to a residence-permit refusal

The small things newcomers get caught on most often. In Ukraine these either aren’t fined at all or the amounts are symbolic.

We couldn’t find a specific article with a fixed fine for drinking in the street: there’s no formal ban on drinking in a park in Georgia, but rowdy drunken behaviour counts as petty hooliganism – and for a foreigner that’s already grounds for deportation.

Drugs deserve a separate mention, because this is where newcomers get caught most often. In Tbilisi you can smell weed on every other corner, which creates a sense of total freedom. In reality, the court in 2017–2018 only legalised consumption itself, and only not in public places and not in front of children. The whole chain up to that point – where you got it, how much you had on you, whom you passed it to – remains punishable, and penalties were increased in 2025. Police use controlled purchases, covert filming and controlled postal deliveries; in the first half of 2026 about 5,000 people were prosecuted.

Scam offices and “office workers”

Who they are, why being around them is dangerous, and seven situations where you need to say “no”

In Dnipro and Kyiv, “offices” is the word for fraudulent call centres. After raids and the new 2026 law, some people from them left, and abroad they often turn out to be generous “acquaintances who’ll sort everything out”: they invite you, house you, help you. This section is about why it’s better not to accept that kind of help.

Who they are. Call-centre operators and managers who phone EU residents “from the bank’s security service” or sell “crypto investments”. Dnipro was the main hub for such offices. Since 18 September 2026, Article 255-4 of Ukraine’s Criminal Code has been in force: participation – up to 10 years with confiscation, recruitment – up to 5 years.

Why it concerns you too. People “from the office” are generous acquaintances, admirers and sponsors: bonuses are spent in the same clubs, residential complexes and chats where newly arrived women go. After the 2026 raids, such an acquaintance is someone whose accounts can be frozen in a day and who faces charges in Ukraine. Everything connected to him – transfers to your card, a flat, a car – ends up in the case file.

More: offices in Georgia and what’s happening to them

Where the offices went – and why Georgia isn’t the main destination. After the raids and the new law, according to Glavcom (September 2026), offices are recruiting people “for the EU” with relocation to Cyprus, Bulgaria, Montenegro and Turkey, while some have gone remote. Investigators don’t see a mass move of Ukrainian offices to Georgia specifically. But Georgia has its own industry of the same origin: Kyiv’s Milton Group and Tbilisi’s Morgan Limited (founded in 2018 by a Ukrainian citizen, €5m taken from victims in Germany, Slovenia and Slovakia in 2019–2021) were one network. According to GI-TOC estimates, Tbilisi has anywhere from 50–100 to “hundreds” of offices, mostly small, 5–10 people each, with a few in Batumi; they recruit through acquaintances rather than ads, operators earn 3,000–5,000 GEL, team leads up to $20–30k a month. Ukrainians here are more often owners, managers and Russian-speaking desks than rank-and-file – and that’s exactly the circle the “acquaintances in Tbilisi” come from.

What changed in Georgia in 2025–2026. Tbilisi’s A.K. Group (OCCRP investigation, March 2025: ≈ 85 employees, $35.3m from 6,100 victims) simply moved to another street after the raids – in August 2026 journalists found it at 68 Mirian Mepe St. But the political cover collapsed: in December 2025 the former head of the State Security Service was arrested for protecting “dozens” of call centres, and in February 2026 a case was opened against the former prosecutor general. In 2021–2024, 89 people and 7 companies were charged – mostly rank-and-file operators, not owners; in a case, a rank-and-file operator is an accomplice, not someone who “just worked there”. At the same time, Georgia deported 3,170 foreigners in January–September 2026 – 2.5 times more than in the same period of 2025 – and extradites suspects to Ukraine. The upshot for you: an office in Georgia today lives under double pressure, and a person “from the office” can lose their money, flat and freedom in a single morning – along with everyone around them.

The real penalty isn’t the fine – it’s the migration service

In short: prostitution itself is an administrative offence in Georgia with a symbolic fine, but for a foreign woman the real penalty isn’t the fine – it’s the migration service. Everything around it (intermediaries, flats, “agencies”) is criminal.

How the law works. Engaging in prostitution falls under Article 172³ of the Code of Administrative Offences: a warning or a fine, in practice tens of lari, more for a repeat within a year. Clients aren’t punished. But Georgia’s Criminal Code hits organisers hard: involving someone in prostitution (Art. 253), facilitating it and providing premises (Art. 254) – up to 6 years, trafficking (Art. 143¹) – 7–20 years. There’s no legalisation, and none is foreseeable – the church and society are against it.

What actually happens. The police don’t hunt individual women; they raid parlours and flats: August 2025 – 13 venues shut down in Tbilisi and Samegrelo, February 2026 – 10 venues and 10 arrests in Tbilisi, with covert filming authorised by a court. Administrators go to prison, and the foreign women working there are checked by the migration service and deported. Bribing a patrol officer, as people are used to doing in Ukraine, won’t work here: attempted bribery is a separate criminal offence.

Conclusion
There’s no “grey zone” in Georgia where police turn a blind eye and clients are polite. There’s a small market, strong stigma and a state that in 2026 solves the problem of foreign women in this field one way – deportation.

Photos, documents and blackmail. In the same chats, blackmail comes up more and more often – over 400 messages in 2024–2026, and the script is almost always the same: someone finds a woman by photo, learns her real name and demands money for silence. Three rules:

  • Face-search services (PimEyes, FaceCheck and similar) use a single photo to find every page with your face – from a profile to a school album. Check yourself before someone else does, and don’t keep recognisable photos next to your real name and number.

Kept women and “sponsors”. A relationship with gifts, a paid-for flat and transfers is neither a crime nor a violation. But four things are worth knowing in advance:

  1. The bank will ask about the source of regular large transfers from a private individual and may close the account.
  2. Such income doesn’t qualify as grounds for a residence permit – after a year you’ll still need a legal basis to stay.
  3. If your partner is Georgian – since June 2026 a special commission checks marriages for residence purposes; a sham marriage means deportation with a ban of up to 10 years.
  4. Georgian families are conservative, and a “sponsor” is most often married – there won’t be any public life together, and promises are just words.

A special case is a sponsor “from the office”, i.e. a fraudulent call centre: his money can vanish along with him after a single raid, and you’ll be asked whose transfers were arriving on your card. More in section 12, “Scam offices and ‘office workers’”.

The main risk for a Ukrainian woman isn’t the police – it’s trafficking. First-month mistakes

Each one costs either money or the right to stay

Key point
Almost all mistakes are about timing: insurance before you fly, 40 days before applying for residence, and first income without a permit.

    Flying in without insurance covering 30,000 GEL (≈ $11,000).
    Since 1 January 2026 it’s checked at boarding and at the border; without a policy your trip ends at the departure airport.
  2. 02
    Renting a flat “on trust”, without a contract.
    A lease is needed for the bank and is mandatory for a residence application – without one, six months later you’ll have to move just for the paperwork.
  3. 03
    Taking a first Georgian order or a bar job “while you’re sorting out papers”.
    Since 1 March 2026 – work permit first, income second; the migration service checks bars and delivery, and from 1 January to 27 October 2026 more than 3,500 foreigners were deported with an entry ban.

    Counting on a visa run.
    Leaving and re-entering formally resets the year, but the border officer decides, and refusals became more common in 2025–2026.
  5. 05
    Putting off the residence question “for later”.
    You must apply no later than 40 days before your legal stay ends, processing takes 10–30 days, and notarised translations and income certificates take weeks to collect.

Residence permits: types and conditions in 2026

Not an obligation, but a way to stay longer than a year

Work and business

Since 1 March 2026 – permit first, income second

Since 1 March 2026, a foreigner without permanent residence can only earn money legally in Georgia under two conditions: a work permit and a residence status – a residence permit or a D1 work visa. This applies to employees, sole traders and the self-employed alike. Those already working for a Georgian company as of 1 March 2026 have a transition period until 1 January 2027.

How the procedure works. You apply online on the Labour Ministry portal (for sole traders and the self-employed – self-employment.moh.gov.ge): a CV, passport, business plan or turnover documents; then a video interview with the Employment Support Agency in Georgian or English. Emails come from self-employment@moh.gov.ge – check your spam. For the first five years the permit is renewed annually; apply no later than 30 days before it expires – after that the system will block the application. The fine for working without a permit is 2,000 GEL, doubled for a repeat offence.

Key point
Remote work for Ukraine or the EU – no permits needed. Your first Georgian client or employer – only after sole-trader registration or a work permit.

The exception for remote workers. If you work fully remotely and only for foreign clients (a Ukrainian or European employer), you don’t need a work permit. But as soon as a Georgian client or employer appears, the rule applies.
